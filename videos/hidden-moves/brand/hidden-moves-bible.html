Hidden Moves

Every ordinary situation is a game. Most people never notice they are playing. This is the case for building a short-form animated brand around everyday game theory: the strategic logic behind ordinary situations. Below are the research, the scoring, and the visual identity.

PHASE 1

Finding an underexploited niche

How I evaluated. I looked for niches where there is clear evidence of demand (large audiences for the subject in long-form video, books or communities) but the presentation, short animated stories with recurring characters, is thin. A niche doesn't count as "untapped" just because I found no competitors. A low competition score here means strong incumbents already own the subject in the format we would use.

Scores are out of 10, and higher is better in every column. For Competition, 10 means little direct competition in short-form animated explainers. For Ease, 10 means easy to research and produce. Audience figures are third-party tracker estimates, linked in Sources. I could not run TikTok search from this environment, so competition scores for TikTok are judgement. Before committing, a one-hour manual audit of TikTok and YouTube search for each top-three niche is a cheap check.

Evidence behind the key scores

  • Game theory is in demand but rarely in short form. Veritasium's prisoner's-dilemma video is around 23M views (third-party trackers). TED-Ed has a lesson on why competing stores cluster (Hotelling's model). The closest format match, Rational Animations (about 460K subscribers), does game-theory and rationality stories in long form and, per one tracker, publishes essentially no Shorts. Freakonomics, the "hidden side of everything" book, reports 4M+ copies sold. I found no recurring-character Shorts brand that explains everyday situations as games.
  • Infrastructure has proven demand and strong incumbents. Practical Engineering has about 4.8M subscribers. Wendover and Half as Interesting (about 2.9M) are adjacent. A newcomer competes with engineers who have credentials and footage.
  • Price mechanics has huge demand and a crowded field. Single episodes of Insider's "So Expensive" have passed 20M views, and business-breakdown Shorts are plentiful.
  • Systems and traffic videos can explode. CGP Grey's "The Simple Solution to Traffic" is around 44M views. The topic pool is narrower, though.
  • Educational animation monetises. Trackers count Brilliant.org sponsoring 300+ YouTube education channels, including Veritasium and Alan Becker. "Explain it to me" demand is broad: r/explainlikeimfive has about 23M members.

The three strongest, compared

Recommendation: Everyday Game Theory

The cast is built into the subject. Game theory is about players, and stickmen make perfect players. Every episode has built-in conflict (two characters want things), a twist (the equilibrium is rarely what you'd guess), and a payoff the viewer can reuse: a named rule like Hotelling's Law or the Winner's Curse. That rule is what makes people feel smarter and share the video.

It absorbs the next two niches. Decoy pricing, supermarket loss leaders, airline overbooking and "free" apps are games companies play with customers. The brand can cover pricing and choice-architecture topics without becoming a generic business or psychology channel. That breadth gives well over 100 ideas without repeating a mechanism.

The risks I'd manage: oversimplifying or misapplying models. Each script would cite a textbook or paper (for example Dixit and Nalebuff's The Art of Strategy) and say "in the simple model" where real markets add complications. Hot-button politics would be used sparingly, for brand safety.

PHASE 2

Brand identity

Channel name

Hidden Moves recommended

Short and international. It describes the payoff of every episode, which is showing the move everyone is making without knowing it. It also works as a verb phrase in titles ("The hidden move behind…").

Alternatives: Payoff, Nash Street, Board Logic, The Game You're In, Smallgames. I have not checked handle or trademark availability. That needs doing before you commit.

Positioning

Everything is a game. Here are the rules.

For curious people who like to understand how the world really works. Each Hidden Moves episode takes a moment you've lived through, like a half-empty train or two gas stations facing each other, finds the players, and reveals the hidden move that explains it. You leave with one rule you'll start noticing everywhere.

Tone: playful, precise, never smug at the viewer. The characters can be smug at each other.

Character design guide

The cast

Pip · Coral
Player one. Optimistic, impulsive, always takes the "obvious" move first. Their mistakes set up the twist.
Dot · Teal
Player two. Dry, observant, half-lidded eyes. Usually spots the hidden move, or gets caught by it so Pip can gloat.
Ref · Yellow
The referee token. Never speaks. Blows the whistle at reveals, holds up rule cards and scoreboards, and carries the kinetic typography.
The Crowd
Faceless grey pawns that show what happens when thousands of people play the same game: queues, traffic, markets.

Proportions and construction

  • Pawn silhouette: a big round head (about 40% of height) sits on a coloured collar disk, like a board-game pawn. You can recognise it as a black silhouette.
  • Body: a short capsule torso in the team colour. Limbs are rubber-hose ink noodles (9 px at 1080 width) with round cream mitten hands, so arms bend in smooth arcs instead of hinging.
  • Face: tall oval eyes with a white glint. The thick eyebrows do most of the acting. A mouth appears only for big emotions, which makes those moments land.
  • Outline: a 6 px ink stroke on everything (characters, props and buildings), so the whole world reads as one toy set.
  • Coloured by side: coral stands left of frame, teal stands right. Viewers learn "coral versus teal" within one episode.

All three are open-licence (OFL) and available on Google Fonts and as npm packages, so the rendering pipeline can bundle them. Kinetic text rule: words snap in on the stressed syllable, numbers count up, and only one line of display type is on screen at a time.

Environments: the Board and the Hidden Layer

The Board. Every story is staged on floating isometric tiles over the dotted cream table. Buildings are chunky toy tokens. The camera dollies between boards like moving pieces.

The Hidden Layer. At the reveal, the tiles flip over to blueprint navy. Cyan lines show the logic: who moves, where the payoffs go, why nobody changes strategy. This flip is the channel's signature.

The Rule Card. Ref blows the whistle and a 2×2 payoff matrix rises from the board as four tiles. Customer shares count up in mono, the stable cell lights up yellow, and the named rule stamps in.

Animation principles

  • Anticipate, then overshoot: 3–4 frames of wind-up before every move. Pops use ease-out-back with about 8% overshoot.
  • Squash and stretch on contact: heads squash 6% on landings, and hands stretch along fast moves.
  • Secondary motion: eyebrows and the collar disk lag 2 frames behind the head. The Crowd moves with 1–3 frame offsets so it feels alive.
  • No dead air: no shot holds still for more than 0.6 s. Something meaningful changes every 1–2 s, and characters blink and breathe while they wait.
  • Camera follows story beats: pushes in on realisations and pulls out on consequences. Background, board and foreground move at 0.3×, 1× and 1.4× for parallax.
  • Signature transitions: Tile Flip (Surface to Hidden Layer), Board Slide (next location), Coin Wipe (a yellow chip rolls across and wipes the frame), Zoom-Through (into a window or token).

Visual consistency rules

  1. Coral is always on the left and teal on the right, except when a story deliberately swaps them.
  2. Yellow is reserved for value: coins, points, the answer, Ref.
  3. Blueprint navy appears only in the Hidden Layer, never as a plain background.
  4. At most three things move at once, and the hero action is always the largest movement.
  5. The hero object fills at least 30% of the frame. Nothing important sits in the bottom 330 px or top 220 px (platform UI).
  6. Captions use one line, Figtree ExtraBold, inside the 1430–1590 px band.
  7. Every episode ends on a Rule Card and a callback to its opening image.
  8. No stock icons, emoji or clip art. Every prop is drawn in the 6 px ink-outline toy style.
